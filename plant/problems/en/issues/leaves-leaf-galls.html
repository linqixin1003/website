Galls

Raised galls on leaves

This is one possible explanation. Compare the signs below with those on your plant.

What to look for

Check whether the swelling is part of the leaf and whether it corresponds to a mark on the other side.

Choose two shoots of similar age, one facing the light and one farther inside the canopy. Follow each from the growing tip back to fully expanded leaves. Record where healthy growth gives way to the change, and whether that point corresponds to a move, a hot spell or a change in care.

Possible causes and next steps

Insects and mites can induce galls. Forms differ among hosts; identify the host and document new growth.

How to tell similar problems apart

A swelling formed from plant tissue stays attached. Compare it with normal buds, graft unions or, on certain legumes, regular root nodules.

Look at the point where the swelling joins the blade. A gall forms part of the leaf and will not lift off like an attached insect. A few firm galls on an otherwise vigorous tree usually call for observation. Check twig swellings separately if shoots beyond them are dying.

What to do now

Photograph the swelling in place and record its position on the plant. Avoid cutting into a main stem or repeatedly disturbing roots just to inspect it.

Inspect several intact swellings before cutting one open. The color and arrangement of intact galls and the identity of the host can help narrow identification. Compare fully expanded leaves of similar age so the normal folds and pale color of an opening bud do not obscure the change.

What to check next

Seek identification if growth weakens or enlarging swellings surround the crown. Include a photo of the entire plant for scale.
