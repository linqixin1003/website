Stem rot

Soft, rotting stems

This is one possible explanation. Compare the signs below with those on your plant.

What to look for

Check whether softening starts at the soil line, and look for discoloration, sunken tissue or injury.

Possible causes and next steps

Persistent moisture can damage the stem base. Pull wet mulch away, prevent water pooling at the crown and inspect the roots.

How to tell similar problems apart

Press lightly beside the damaged area. Soft collapse can follow a bruise, chilling or decay; spreading softness is more concerning than a stable dent.

Start at the point where the stem meets the mix and look upward, moving loose fallen leaves aside. Note the lowest firm section and whether the soft area is on one side or wraps around the stem. A shoot can still have green leaves even when the base is too weak to support their weight.

What to do now

Remove detached, decaying material and clean tools before touching healthy tissue. Keep healthy parts away from leaking damaged tissue.

If the pot is wet inside a decorative cover, take it out and let it drain freely. Check that water can leave through the holes. A dry surface over a wet root ball is common enough to warrant checking below the surface before watering. Keep the stem base visible instead of adding mix to prop up a collapsing shoot.

What to check next

Watch where firm tissue meets the soft patch. If softness reaches the stem base or develops on several plants, seek identification promptly.

If a single stem was bruised during transport, compare the edge with its original photo after it dries. A firm, stable edge can be left alone. Rapid softening into neighboring stems or collapse at the crown calls for prompt diagnosis; keep that pot’s runoff away from other plants.
