Tiny white adults lift off when a leaf is turned

Greenhouse whiteflies spend much of their development attached beneath a leaf. The familiar cloud of little white adults shows where to begin searching, but it gives only part of the picture. Comparing younger and older leaves helps reveal a colony that survives after the flying adults disperse.

Where to look

Disturb one leaf gently and watch where the white insects settle. Turn over an older leaf on the same plant to inspect the underside with a lens. When checking a sticky card, trace the catch back to plants immediately around it. The card cannot tell you which leaf has an established colony; finding attached immature stages is the part that links the insects to a particular plant.

What confirms it

Look for flattened oval immatures attached to the lower surface. Adults and developing young on one plant provide stronger evidence than a single flying insect.

Similar damage

Fungus gnats usually rise from potting mix and have darker bodies. Sticky foliage or dark mold can accompany whiteflies but occurs with other sap feeders too.

What to do

In a greenhouse or indoors, separate infested plants and inspect adjacent foliage. Yellow sticky cards help detect flying adults; examine leaves to find the young.

Follow-up

Revisit the same group of plants and turn over several leaves of different ages. Record live young as well as adults caught near the canopy.

Check a lower leaf with attached immature whiteflies even when few adults rise from the plant. Then examine the undersides of leaves above it. A drop in flying adults after trapping is encouraging, but fresh colonies on those leaves show that the plant still needs follow-up.
