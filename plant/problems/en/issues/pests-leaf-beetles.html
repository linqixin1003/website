Leaf beetles

Oval beetles and chewed leaves

This is one possible explanation. Compare the signs below with those on your plant.

What to look for

Photograph the wing covers, body shape and the actual feeding site.

Possible causes and next steps

Some beetles are predators. Confirm the host and observe feeding before deciding whether control is needed.

How to tell similar problems apart

Watch for a hard-bodied beetle actually chewing the plant. A beetle resting on a flower may be visiting for pollen.

Inspect a fresh bite, then look on the reverse of that leaf and on the shoot directly above it. Record whether the tissue is eaten right through or just scraped away in thin windows. Photograph both the beetle and the plant it is feeding on. The striped beetle in the photograph is one member of a large family; leaf beetles on other hosts can look quite different.

What to do now

Look at fresh feeding edges and the undersides of adjacent leaves. Note whether damage is confined to petals or also affects new leaves.

Watch what happens at an intact edge before removing an insect. A ladybird searching among aphids may be walking beside holes left by something else. If you see active chewing on a seedling with only a few leaves, check every growing tip. On a well-established plant with scattered old holes, keep the remaining green leaves and compare newly opened ones before intervening.

What to check next

Compare the next flush of leaves with a photo. If buds repeatedly fail to open, collect close-ups of both damage and beetle.
