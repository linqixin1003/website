Pear-shaped insects on shoots

Inspect tender shoots for groups of pear-shaped insects. With a hand lens, look for two small tubes near the rear of the abdomen. Adult aphids can have wings, so a flying individual does not rule them out.

What to look for

Check young shoots and buds for clusters, cast skins and sticky foliage.

Possible causes and next steps

These signs fit aphids. Gently rinse a small colony away and look for predators such as lady beetles.

How to tell similar problems apart

Clusters on soft shoots, shed white skins and sticky leaves point towards aphids. White skins alone may remain after the insects have gone.

Choose a tip where the youngest leaves are just opening. Look along its underside, then follow the stem down to the next pair of leaves. If a lower leaf feels sticky, inspect the shoot directly above it. Honeydew can fall onto a healthy leaf, so cleaning the sticky patch alone may leave the colony in place.

What to do now

Support a sturdy shoot and rinse its tip and leaf undersides gently. Check tender shoots on nearby plants too.

On a small indoor plant, check a few buds by gently parting the leaves without forcing the bud open. Remove insects from reachable surfaces and note any cluster you could not reach. Outdoors, look for ladybird larvae or lacewing larvae among the aphids. If predators are active and the tip is still growing, revisit that shoot before deciding on further control.

What to check next

Inspect newly unfolding leaves for live colonies. Old curled leaves may stay curled even after the aphids are gone.

Compare two tips after rinsing: one that had aphids and an uncurled tip beside it. If only empty skins remain on the first but a cluster has appeared on the second, direct attention to the new colony. Check that the insects actually have bodies under the pale outlines.
