Young pears swell rapidly and blacken

A fruitlet that grows faster than all its neighbours after flowering may carry midge larvae. The inside of the pear reveals more than its unusual shape alone.

What to look for

Compare newly set pears in one cluster. The first warning can be an oversized, unusually round fruitlet that looks further advanced than its neighbours. It later blackens, splits or falls.

Check the pattern

Cut open a suspect fruitlet over a light surface. Multiple pale larvae inside a damaged centre strongly support pear midge. A frost-blackened flower centre without larvae calls for a different explanation.

What to do

Pick swollen fruitlets early, before they open and release larvae. Gather newly fallen pieces too. Affected pears cannot resume normal growth, so concentrate on protecting the remaining crop.

When to check again

Mark the affected tree and inspect it again immediately after flowering next year. Compare trees at a similar bloom stage rather than comparing fruit size between early and late varieties.

Catch the short observation window

Choose a swollen pear before it has split. Cutting recently fallen black fruit may reveal only an empty cavity because larvae can already have left. Photograph an intact affected pear beside a normal fruitlet before opening it to show the early size difference.
