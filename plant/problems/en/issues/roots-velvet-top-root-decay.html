Yellow-rimmed rosettes beside conifer roots

A low rosette beside a conifer can grow from a woody root below the soil. Young velvet-top fruiting bodies often have a bright yellow growing edge around a darker, soft-looking center.

Inspect the specimen

Photograph the fresh upper surface and, if visible without disturbing the root zone, the pores beneath it. Older rosettes turn dark brown and can resemble a lump of soil or a weathered piece of wood.

Check similar symptoms

Record the nearest live conifer and any remaining stumps. A mushroom appearing on the lawn may be attached to a buried dead root; the nearest trunk is not automatically its host.

Choose a response

Where the growth is associated with a standing tree, especially beside a structure, seek an arborist's evaluation of root and butt decay. The size or number of rosettes cannot measure the strength of the remaining wood.

Track the next change

Mark the location on a sketch and note whether a new rosette appears there next season. A dark previous year's fruiting body and a new yellow one may represent the same underlying decay.

Host-specific check

Include the distance and compass direction from the trunk to the rosette. As fallen needles cover the previous season's fruiting body, this record can help relocate it.

Where the rosette seems to emerge from lawn rather than bark, include nearby conifers and old stumps in a wide photo. Buried wood can explain that position. Avoid digging across structural roots to chase the connection; let a qualified arborist assess the tree in place.
