Dark shoestring-like cords at the root collar

Dark cords around roots are a different clue from mushrooms in a lawn. Trace what they are attached to before deciding whether a nearby tree is involved or only buried wood is being colonized.

Inspect the plant

Inspect naturally loose bark or exposed roots for branching dark cords. White fan-shaped fungal growth beneath loose bark is another useful clue to Armillaria. If a plant has already been lifted, keep a piece of affected root attached to its bark for the diagnostic service. Avoid digging around sound structural roots of a standing tree.

Distinguish similar signs

Fine roots have a different branching texture. Mushrooms alone may arise from buried dead wood, so document their connection to the plant before drawing conclusions.

Choose a response

Avoid stripping healthy bark to search for fans. If a declining tree shows cords at the root collar, request a root and structural inspection.

Follow up

Record neighboring stumps and recent tree removals along with crown changes. The fungus can persist in woody material beneath the soil.

A detail to record

Where a living tree stands beside an old stump, photograph the cords on each separately. Evidence on the stump establishes colonization there; a living root with fans beneath loose bark is a separate finding.

Observe cords already exposed on loosened bark or a dead stump rather than peeling sound bark from a standing tree. A cord crossing a root surface and decayed wood at the root collar are different observations; tell an arborist which you actually found.
