A zigzag bite starts at the margin of an elm leaf

Young elm zigzag sawflies cut a distinctive path between the side veins of an elm leaf. Lightly affected leaves can be more useful for identification than severely eaten ones. When inspecting a branch, save a photograph of the first narrow zigzag as well as the broader loss of foliage.

Where to look

First confirm the tree is an elm. On lightly damaged leaves, follow the narrow zigzag feeding track from the margin inward between veins. Place a sheet of contrasting paper behind the leaf when photographing it. The open zigzag can otherwise blend into the green foliage behind it, making a surface discoloration and a missing piece of leaf look misleadingly similar.

What confirms it

Turn the leaf over and inspect its growing edge for a small green sawfly larva. Older larvae consume larger areas and can erase the original pattern.

Similar damage

A leafminer leaves some leaf skin above its gallery. The sawfly removes the full thickness along an open path starting at the leaf margin.

What to do

Photograph fresh feeding and the larva together. If this is a new local finding, consult the regional plant-health service for identification and local guidance.

Follow-up

Inspect fresh growth on more than one branch. Record whether injury stays scattered or repeatedly removes a large share of the canopy.
