Bladder galls on maple leaves

Maple bladder galls are rounded swellings in the leaf tissue. The photograph shows young green galls. As the same galls mature, they can turn pink, red and eventually black.

Inspect the specimen

Each bump is continuous with the leaf, raised tissue rather than an insect resting on top. Count the bumps on one leaf, then compare a neighbor.

Check similar symptoms

View the leaf from the side. A red or black bump that rises above the blade can be a mature bladder gall. A flat brown spot or a long, narrow projection has a different shape and needs a separate identification.

Choose a response

On a healthy established maple, leave functioning leaves in place even as the galls darken. Picking the bumps off tears the leaf tissue.

Track the next change

Mark a leaf bearing a few galls and photograph it again later. Compare the number and shape of the swellings. A green gall turning red at the same spot records a color change, while a bump on a newly unfolded leaf records another affected leaf.

Host-specific check

Record this only on maple leaves. Check the leaf shape before adding a gall from another tree.

Compare leaves from the same shoot that unfolded at different times. Galls confined to the earlier flush document feeding when those leaves were developing. Once a full-sized leaf bears a bladder, washing or picking at the bump will not restore its flat outline.

If a maple has both curled foliage and bladder galls, compare leaves with and without the bumps on the same branch. Curling that also affects gall-free leaves deserves its own investigation. Removing a handful of galled leaves cannot explain or correct a change shared across the shoot.
