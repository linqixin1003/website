Wedge-shaped jumping insects

The insect is wedge-shaped and jumps when touched. Pear-shaped insects crowded on a soft shoot, and not quick to jump, belong with the aphid guide page.

What to look for

Disturb a leaf lightly and watch for sideways jumping. Photograph the body and faded areas of the leaf.

Possible causes and next steps

Leafhopper feeding can cause pale areas. Compare new growth before attributing damage to a visiting insect.

How to tell similar problems apart

Leafhoppers tend to spring sideways or away when disturbed. Yellowing by itself has many other causes.

Before touching the plant, look along a leaf held at eye level. These small insects often rest with the wings sloping over a narrow body; nymphs lack fully developed wings. If the insect springs away, turn the neighbouring leaves slowly. A photograph taken from the side while it is resting is more useful than counting the insects that fly out when a whole branch is shaken.

What to do now

Approach leaf undersides slowly and photograph a resting insect before disturbing the shoot.

Compare an affected leaf with one of the same age on a different shoot. Note whether the damage is scattered pinpricks, a browned margin, or distortion of the newest growth. Similar appearances can follow heat or root stress. A single jumping insect does not establish the cause of a whole plant turning yellow. If symptoms continue to spread, record the host, leaf age and pattern for identification.

What to check next

Check for fresh feeding marks on young leaves. If distortion progresses after insects leave, seek a host-specific diagnosis.

Examine the underside of several leaves before shaking a shoot. Adults jump readily and may be visitors; small wingless nymphs running sideways across leaves are easier to associate with the plant where they were found. Inspect a second shoot before judging how widespread the population is.
