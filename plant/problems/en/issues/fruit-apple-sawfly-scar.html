A winding ribbon scar crosses an apple

A narrow feeding track made when an apple is tiny can grow into a broad corky ribbon. Check the younger fruitlets for signs of more recent injury.

What to look for

Follow the scar with your eye. A young apple sawfly larva can feed just below the skin, leaving a ribbon-like trail that broadens as the surviving apple grows.

Check the pattern

Inspect nearby fruitlets for deeper holes and dark waste. A single bramble scratch lacks this combination. A tunnel detected only late in a ripe apple can also be caused by codling moth.

What to do

Collect newly damaged fruitlets and early drops before larvae move on. A superficial old scar on a firm growing apple marks earlier feeding and is not evidence that a larva is still there.

When to check again

Check shortly after petal fall next spring. Photograph early mines while they are still narrow and record which trees show damage. Later, compare the surviving marked fruit with that early record.

Follow the fruit from petal fall

Check the smallest fruits while the skin is still smooth elsewhere. Outline the end of a fresh track in a photograph and revisit the same fruit. A surviving apple can carry a long scar even when the original injury occupied only a small part of the fruitlet.

Inspect a cluster before thinning it. If one fruitlet has a ribbon scar, examine its immediate neighbours for a fresh entry hole. Recording the cluster first avoids losing the link between an old surface mine and newer deep damage on a nearby fruit.
