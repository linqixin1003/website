Small knots formed within feeder roots

When a vegetable stays small despite watering, inspect a declining plant before it dies. Root-knot nematodes can cause swellings along fine roots, leaving a plant poorly equipped to take up water during afternoon heat.

Inspect the specimen

If you plan to send a sample, keep the living roots and surrounding soil together and follow the receiving laboratory’s instructions. Leave the sample unwashed unless the laboratory asks otherwise. On a separate root, look for swellings that form within the root tissue.

Check similar symptoms

Peas and beans also produce beneficial nitrogen-fixing nodules, which often attach to the side of a root. The patterns can coexist, so a photograph of a single bump cannot identify a nematode species.

Choose a response

Avoid moving suspect soil to new beds. If rotation or a resistant cultivar is being considered, ask a local lab how to submit living roots and soil from the edge of the affected area.

Track the next change

Map which crops show galls and which neighboring plants remain vigorous. Keep host and cultivar labels with each sample; resistance to some root-knot species does not cover every species.

Host-specific check

On tomato, sample fine branching roots as well as thick roots near the stem. Looking only at the crown can miss many small, scattered swellings farther out.

On legumes, distinguish nodules attached to the side of a root from swellings formed within the root. Follow the root axis through the lump and inspect more than one root; healthy nitrogen-fixing nodules can coexist with an unrelated problem elsewhere on the plant.
