Iris leaves streak and rhizomes hollow out

Iris borer injury changes position as the caterpillar grows. In spring the clue can be a single damaged leaf. By the time a whole fan wilts, the insect may already be feeding inside the fleshy underground stem. Inspect the leaf and its attached rhizome together to reconstruct that progression.

Where to look

Start at the base of a damaged iris fan. Follow a water-soaked streak down the leaf and look for ragged tissue and material pushed out by a feeding larva. If a rhizome is already entirely decomposed, choose a second fan at the edge of the declining clump. Following a less advanced lesion can preserve both the feeding track and the boundary with sound tissue.

What confirms it

Lift one declining fan and inspect its fleshy rhizome. A pinkish caterpillar or a tunnel connects early leaf injury to later underground damage.

Similar damage

A foul smell points to secondary soft rot. Smell alone cannot identify the insect that opened the wound.

What to do

Remove badly hollowed rhizomes. In fall, clear dead iris foliage where eggs can survive the winter.

Follow-up

In spring inspect the emerging fans while larvae are still inside the leaves. Mark affected clumps so the rhizomes can be checked again.

When dividing a damaged iris, trace each tunnel from the leaf base into the rhizome before separating divisions. Set aside any piece showing active tunneling or soft decay. A sound neighboring fan can be checked on its own rather than discarded because another fan in the clump collapsed.
