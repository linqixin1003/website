Brown knobbly clusters persist on ash trees

Dry brown masses that become conspicuous in winter can be old male flowers altered by mites. Check their shape and the health of the surrounding crown.

What to look for

Look up into an ash canopy after leaf fall. Rough brown bunches may remain attached long after the leaves have dropped. They formed from male flowers earlier in the year.

Check the pattern

Check the shape of any nearby seeds. Ordinary ash keys have flat elongated wings. Flower galls are irregular rounded masses, initially green, which turn brown as the season progresses.

What to do

On an otherwise vigorous tree, leave the clusters in place. Minute mites caused the developing flowers to swell, and cutting healthy branches to remove old galls is unlikely to benefit the tree.

When to check again

Revisit the tree at flowering and look for fresh green clusters. Track canopy thinning or dead branches separately. Those changes call for their own assessment and cannot be explained from persistent flower galls alone.

Mark this year's growth

Look for green clusters on twigs that carried male flowers in spring and dry brown ones on older growth. Photograph both colours together if possible. This prevents last year's persistent galls from being counted as fresh damage every time you inspect the tree.
