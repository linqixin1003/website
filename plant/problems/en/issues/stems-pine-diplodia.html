Stunted pine candles beside cones with black specks

When several pine shoots turn brown, the length of their needles helps establish when growth stopped. An affected candle, a healthy candle and an older cone together make a more informative sample than loose brown needles.

Inspect the plant

Compare the new candles on adjacent pine shoots. Diplodia can kill a shoot while its needles are still short; resin may accumulate near its base. Look at cone scales still on nearby branches and note the height of the first affected shoots. These observations help a clinic interpret a close photograph of black dots.

Distinguish similar signs

With a lens, inspect older cones for tiny black fruiting structures. Brown needles of normal length on an otherwise healthy shoot suggest another pattern.

Choose a response

Prune accessible dead shoots in dry conditions and avoid overhead watering. Check stress from drought or injury when several lower branches are involved.

Follow up

Record both the affected needle length and the timing of candle development next spring. A clinic can examine a shoot and a nearby cone together.

A detail to record

Compare two shoots from the same pine branch whorl. If one candle stopped elongating while its neighbor produced a full set of needles, record the short candle's length rather than averaging the two.

Compare two seasons on the same twig

Locate the bud-scale scars between last year’s wood and this year’s candle. Compare the length and needles of several shoots in that whorl. A short current-season shoot holding dead needles differs from fading across many ages of needles along an entire branch. Include the transition between seasons and a nearby cone in photographs for identification.
