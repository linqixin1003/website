Recognizing spongy moth egg masses

The photograph shows the egg mass on bark, not a caterpillar feeding. Look separately for larvae and chewed leaves.

Spongy moth caterpillars can remove much of an oak’s canopy. The caterpillars do the feeding; the adult moths leave a different clue for next season, a patch of eggs covered with pale tan hairs.

Where to look

Inspect rough bark beneath a thinning crown during the day. Larger caterpillars can shelter there before returning to feed at night. Droppings below branches help locate the active part of a large tree.

What confirms it

On a well-grown caterpillar look for pairs of blue bumps followed by red pairs along the back. A close photograph is preferable to handling a hairy caterpillar with bare skin.

Similar damage

Tent caterpillars gather in or around a silk shelter. With spongy moth, search the bark, nearby sheltered surfaces and outdoor objects for the characteristic feltlike egg masses instead.

What to do

Scrape reachable egg masses into a container of soapy water and leave them submerged for several days. Simply brushing a mass onto the ground can leave viable eggs behind.

Follow-up

Track whether the tree produces new foliage after feeding ends. Record repeated heavy defoliation and have extensive damage across multiple mature trees assessed by a local tree professional.

When the crown looks thin, check rough bark in daylight for resting larger caterpillars and look upward again after dusk. If feeding has ended and moths or egg masses are present, shift attention to mapping reachable egg masses rather than repeatedly searching bare leaves for larvae.
