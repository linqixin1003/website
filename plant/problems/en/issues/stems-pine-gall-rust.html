Round woody swellings on pine branches

A permanent wooden ball on a pine shoot can become much more conspicuous when spring spores appear. Its location on a fine lateral or on the central leader determines how much of the tree is at stake.

Inspect the plant

Check whether the swelling is continuous with the pine branch. Gall rust can produce a nearly spherical enlargement that persists from one year to the next. Look for a bend or dead tip beyond the gall. Add a photograph of healthy needles and a cone if available, since identifying the pine helps narrow the rust diagnosis.

Distinguish similar signs

Look after spring rains for orange spore masses on an older gall. A normal cone has distinct scales and attaches to the branch at a point.

Choose a response

If limited to a small branch, remove the gall-bearing branch before the spring spore display. Trunk galls require individual evaluation.

Follow up

Note the pine species and photograph the same gall across seasons. Several gall rusts look alike and identification may need additional host evidence.

A detail to record

Keep several intact pine needle bundles in the host photograph and record how many needles emerge from each sheath. A handful of loose needles loses this identification clue.

Locate the swelling relative to the trunk

Trace the branch through the swelling and note whether wood emerges at both ends. Estimate the distance to the trunk junction. Two equally large galls can have very different implications if one is near a tip and the other surrounds the main stem. Do not shave a trunk gall off as though it were material stuck to the bark.
