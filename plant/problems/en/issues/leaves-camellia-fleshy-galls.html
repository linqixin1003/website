Camellia leaves become thick, fleshy and white

A pale lump at the end of a camellia shoot may have started as a single leaf. Recognizing it while the tissue is still fleshy gives a gardener a practical opportunity to remove individual galls.

Inspect the plant

Check the new flush on camellias, especially sasanqua. Part or all of a tender leaf can swell into a pale, heavy lump that no longer resembles a flat leaf. Trace the lump back to its leaf stalk and inspect the neighboring bud. This separates a distorted leaf from a damaged flower that happens to rest on the foliage.

Distinguish similar signs

The white coating develops on tissue that is already swollen. Compare with a normal-thickness leaf carrying superficial powder or scale insects.

Choose a response

Pick off a few reachable galls while still firm and before their surface becomes powdery. Remove them from the planting instead of leaving them underneath.

Follow up

Examine other soft shoots while they expand, particularly after a wet spell. Old brown galls can persist after the infection window has passed.

A detail to record

On a camellia with only a few swollen leaves, mark one neighboring shoot whose leaves have not yet unfolded. Revisit it after expansion to distinguish newly formed galls from old ones hidden by the canopy.

Find where the swollen tissue begins

Before removing a gall, examine the other leaves on the same new shoot. If only one blade has become a thick pale mass and its neighbors are normally shaped, record that distribution. Uniform thick foliage throughout an established camellia calls for checking normal cultivar traits first.
