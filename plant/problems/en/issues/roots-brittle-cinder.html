Gray crusts turning charcoal-black at a tree base

Low, flat growth at a root flare is easy to overlook beside showier shelf fungi. Its position puts the supporting base of the tree at issue, even if foliage remains green.

Inspect the plant

Check the root flare and spaces between major roots. Young Kretzschmaria crusts have pale margins and gray centers; older growth resembles brittle charcoal. A close-up records the crust but leaves out the target area. Include a wider photograph showing the tree, nearby paths and the position of the fungal growth relative to ground level.

Distinguish similar signs

Note whether the material is attached to a living tree base or to a separate dead stump. Surface color alone cannot identify the extent of internal decay.

Choose a response

Keep people and vehicles away from the fall zone if the affected tree could strike a target. Arrange an assessment by a qualified arborist.

Follow up

If the tree has developed a fresh lean, cracking or movement at the root plate, keep clear and report it promptly. Take any photos from outside the area the tree or a limb could strike; the arborist can examine the root flare up close.

A detail to record

The shaded gaps between buttress roots can hide low fungal crusts in a front view. If those areas cannot be seen safely from a distance, tell the arborist which side was visible and leave the rest for the assessment.

Where a dark crust lies between buttress roots, photograph its outline against the bark and the adjacent ground. A thin crust can conceal a much larger structural problem. If a tree overhangs a path or building, arrange a professional assessment before anyone works beneath it.
