Orange pear leaf spots with horns underneath

Turn a spotted pear leaf over and look directly beneath the orange patch. The two surfaces provide different clues.

What to check first

Choose a leaf still attached to the tree. Photograph its upper surface, then turn it over without losing track of the same spot. Early in the season you may see a bright orange patch and tiny dark points above, with little to see below.

How to tell similar problems apart

Later the underside can swell and develop a cluster of small raised cones. Their sides split into a lattice as they mature. A rough olive scab or a brown sun-scorched edge does not have this paired arrangement. If only orange color is present, keep the identification provisional.

What to do now

Check how much healthy foliage remains before deciding what to remove. Stripping many spotted leaves at once reduces the leaf area of a small tree. Note whether a nearby juniper has unusual swellings; its presence is useful background, but does not by itself identify the cause.

What to check next

Revisit a marked shoot later in summer. Compare both faces of the affected leaves and record whether leaves are dropping early. If a young tree repeatedly loses much of its foliage, send paired photographs and the timing of leaf fall to a local horticultural adviser.

Track the two surfaces of one pear leaf

Mark a leaf with a new orange spot while it is still attached. Later in the season, check the underside of that same spot for projecting structures. A photograph taken too early can miss them; pair the two views before deciding that an unrelated leaf spot is pear rust.
