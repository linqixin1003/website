Cranberries contain white cottony tissue

Poorly coloured berries may contain an unexpected white filling. Inspect fruit interiors together with the history of spring shoot damage in the same patch.

What to look for

Cut open a suspect cranberry that has remained pale or turned tan while neighbouring berries ripen. White cottony material inside the fruit is an important clue to cottonball disease.

Check the pattern

Examine where the white material lies. Surface mould on a bruised berry is less specific. Pay attention to poorly coloured fruit and to the condition of nearby upright shoots as well.

What to do

Set affected berries aside during picking and document their location in the bed. Take a photo of a fresh cross section before the berry dries, with a healthy berry for comparison.

When to check again

Next spring, revisit that patch as new shoots appear. Ask a cranberry specialist to assess recurring tip blight and internal fruit symptoms together, particularly if losses extend beyond a few scattered berries.

Keep track of the affected patch

Mark a corner of the bed near the first affected berries. At the next harvest, inspect a handful from inside that patch and another from outside it. Cutting a small sample can reveal hidden internal changes that surface colour alone misses.

Compare berries that stayed attached to sound uprights with overripe fruit lying in wet litter. Keeping the samples apart preserves the distinction between a change inside a developing berry and decay that appeared after it touched the ground.
