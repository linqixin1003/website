White powder on plant surfaces

The white film wipes off and the leaf surface remains. The same film on a stem belongs with the stem guide page. A white cover that will not wipe off is not this page.

What to look for

Look for irregular powdery patches and check leaves, young stems and fruit.

Examine the boundary of the white area. Powdery patches may spread beyond their first spot; residue from a dried droplet can leave a distinct ring. Check a second leaf before choosing treatment.

Possible causes and next steps

Powdery mildew and surface residues can look similar. Note recurrence, allow airflow and identify the host.

How to tell similar problems apart

Natural wax often coats similar surfaces evenly. Powdery growth more often forms irregular patches that enlarge or appear on fresh tissue.

Observe where the white patches begin. Circular colonies spreading across previously clean tissue deserve a mildew check. Evenly spaced rings after hard-water misting may be deposits. Look at fresh growth away from the spray path and avoid repeatedly rubbing tender leaves to test them.

What to do now

Compare affected and healthy surfaces on the same plant. Photograph the coating before touching it, and leave healthy fruit wax intact.

Look at the boundary around a fresh patch before wiping. Note whether the patch grows outward on the same living blade between two undisturbed observations. Keep a photo of the original surface so you can distinguish a new deposit from one missed during cleaning.

What to check next

If new patches spread to shoots or leaves, identify the host and seek advice on whether treatment is needed.

Compare where the white material starts

A white patch spreading across live tissue in irregular islands differs from hard rings left at the margins of dried droplets. Examine protected leaves that have never received overhead spray as well as exposed ones. On hairy foliage, use a lens so natural leaf hairs are not mistaken for mildew.
