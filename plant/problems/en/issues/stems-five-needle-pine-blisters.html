One branch on a five-needle pine turns rusty red

A red branch stands out against a green crown. Follow it inward and inspect the bark as well as the needles.

What to check first

First check a healthy twig. The white pines affected by blister rust carry needles in bundles of five. On the discolored branch, look back toward the trunk for a patch of damaged or swollen bark. Photograph the bark and the point where green needles give way to red ones.

How to tell similar problems apart

In spring, pale blisters on the bark can reveal orange material. They are seasonal, so an autumn inspection may show only rough bark or resin. Normal shedding affects older needles inside several branches. A whole red tip beyond one bark lesion has a different pattern. A snapped or girdled branch can also turn red.

What to do now

Record whether the injury is confined to a side branch or reaches the main stem. That distinction affects whether pruning could remove the affected tissue. For a trunk lesion or a branch too high to inspect from the ground, have an arborist assess the tree before cutting.

What to check next

Keep a photograph of the whole crown as a baseline. At the next inspection compare nearby green shoots and check for a second flagged branch. Mark the location on a sketch so the same patch of bark can be inspected again when blisters are most visible.

Compare a flagged branch with a whole row of pines

Step back and compare the row. One red branch beyond an isolated bark injury deserves a close photograph of that junction. Discoloration at the same height on the windward side of several trees suggests a shared exposure worth reporting, such as drying winds or roadside salt spray. A close-up of the red needles alone loses that information.
