Sheets of fungal tissue under shed oak bark

Broad plaques revealed beneath oak bark deserve a closer look when they follow prolonged drought. Establish whether the plaque is on a dead limb, a still-living limb or the main trunk.

Inspect the plant

Look along the trunk and large limbs where the outer bark has already fallen. Exposed fungal layers can change from powdery brown to hard gray or black. Check the root zone for compacted soil, trenching or altered drainage. Include these observations in the assessment because supporting a stressed neighboring oak may require different action from managing a dead limb.

Distinguish similar signs

Record recent drought or root disturbance. These fungi are often evident on stressed or dying wood, so the exposed mat alone cannot date the original injury.

Choose a response

Leave intact bark in place. If extensive sheets occur on a trunk or over a path, move activities away and seek a tree-risk assessment.

Follow up

Inspect neighboring oaks for thinning crowns and moisture stress. Photograph new areas of bark loss separately from older exposed wood.

A detail to record

If only one side of the oak is shedding bark, note which major limbs originate above that face and whether they still carry foliage. Include the opposite, intact face in the tree assessment.
