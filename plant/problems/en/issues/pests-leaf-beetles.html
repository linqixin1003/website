Identifying striped leaf beetles

Use the photograph to recognize the insect: an oval body, many longitudinal stripes, resting on a green leaf. Do not open this page for a round hole alone.

Inspect the specimen

See whether the stripes run the length of the wing covers and whether the legs and antennae are present. If the stripes do not match, do not treat it as the same beetle.

Check similar symptoms

A cucumber beetle is narrower, usually with three stripes on yellow wing covers. This beetle is rounder and has more stripes.

Choose a response

Before removing a striped beetle, identify the host plant and look for fresh feeding on it. If an adult is simply resting beside an old hole, photograph it and recheck the leaf before deciding on control.

Track the next change

Note whether the beetle is on the upper surface, the underside, or the leaf stalk. A change of position still needs the same stripes.

Host-specific check

Use this page for this beetle on a leaf. A long-horned beetle on stone or bark is not this insect.

Hold a container below the leaf before disturbing an adult. After removal, photograph the nearest intact leaf edge. Compare it at the next inspection: a new notch beside the original feeding site is better evidence of continued chewing than an old hole stretched by leaf expansion.

Before choosing a beetle treatment, identify the plant carrying the insect. A striped adult on a potato leaf and a narrower beetle inside a squash blossom call for different checks. A useful specimen record includes the whole host plant and a clear view of the beetle's back.
