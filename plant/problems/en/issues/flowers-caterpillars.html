Caterpillars

Caterpillars on flowers

This is one possible explanation. Compare the signs below with those on your plant.

What to look for

Look behind petals and around buds for larvae, irregular bites or pellet-like droppings.

Possible causes and next steps

Holes alone cannot identify a pest. Photograph the insect and its feeding site before acting. Leave undamaged blooms in place.

How to tell similar problems apart

Look inside folded leaves for a soft larva and loose droppings. Slugs can make similar holes but may leave a shiny trail. Check beneath the flower for fallen pellets before moving the pot or rinsing the foliage.

Start with a bud that is just beginning to open. A caterpillar can feed through several folded petals at once, leaving a row of matching holes when the flower opens. A ragged bloom may therefore show damage from days ago. Look for pellets beneath a still-closed bud to find a better place to search.

What to do now

Inspect buds and both leaf surfaces at dusk. Move an identified nuisance caterpillar with a tool; avoid handling hairy larvae.

Hold a light behind the bud and look around the green sepals where they meet the petals. If a larva is visible, take a picture beside the flower to show its size. On a plant grown for butterflies, a few chewed blooms may be an acceptable part of growing the host plant.

What to check next

New holes with fresh droppings mean feeding continues. Photograph the larva before deciding whether to intervene further.

Choose two intact buds on the same stem and note their positions. Check again when they open. If both open cleanly and the old holes are unchanged, removal of more flowers serves little purpose.
