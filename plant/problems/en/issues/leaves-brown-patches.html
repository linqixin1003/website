Brown patches on leaves

The brown areas have real width. Pinpoint brown dots belong with the fine-speckle guide page, and a brown spot ringed with yellow belongs with the yellow-halo guide page.

What to look for

Compare window-facing and sheltered leaves. Note whether patches follow the margin, lie between veins or occur at random.

Compare two leaves at the same height, one facing the window and one shaded by other foliage. Browning confined to the exposed side makes a recent change in light worth checking. Similar patches on shaded leaves call for a wider search.

Possible causes and next steps

Sun injury, moisture stress and leaf diseases can each leave brown patches. Review changes in light and photograph a healthy new leaf for comparison.

How to tell similar problems apart

Compare the edge of a patch with nearby healthy tissue. One mark where a tie or another leaf rubbed suggests an injury; scattered new marks deserve closer inspection.

Map the patches against the light source. Broad papery areas restricted to newly exposed leaves suggest checking for a recent jump in sunlight. Small spots developing on sheltered leaves as well deserve closer inspection, particularly where nearby leaves touch when wet.

What to do now

Photograph the same patch with a ruler beside it. Water the soil directly so the surface can dry between waterings.

Before pruning, check the opposite side of the plant at the same height. The distribution may reveal which leaves were shaded by branches or neighboring pots. If you request identification, include the plant name and both views of an early lesion while there is still green tissue around it.

What to check next

If the patch grows or marks appear on new growth, bring photos showing the whole plant and both sides of the affected tissue.

Is the original spot expanding, or are new leaves being affected?

Choose two leaves on the same shoot: one already spotted and the youngest fully expanded leaf that is still clear. Use a vein junction as a fixed landmark when photographing the original patch. At the next check, record a changed margin on the first leaf separately from any new lesion on the second. A single dead patch drying and shrinking tells a different story from injury appearing on a new flush.

Do I need to remove the first two spotted leaves?

If only two older leaves are marked, the margins are dry and new foliage is clean, keep one affected leaf for comparison. Look for spots on newly opened leaves rather than repeatedly trimming dead brown tissue. If fresh spots appear on several branches after wet weather, avoid splashing water between plants and keep a leaf with an intact spot margin for identification.
