White powder on plant surfaces

The white film is on the stem and wipes off. The same film on a leaf belongs with the leaf guide page.

What to look for

Inspect the underside of a young shoot and the area beside each bud. Record whether the white material forms a continuous film or small separate tufts at the joints.

Possible causes and next steps

Check an untouched shoot before attributing the film to mildew. Spray residue can follow drip lines, while some plants naturally carry a pale waxy surface on otherwise healthy stems.

How to tell similar problems apart

Compare the newest internode with an older one on the same shoot. If the film is even on matching young growth across several stems, record that pattern before wiping it away.

Angle the shoot toward the light and compare the coating on old and newly opened leaves. Look at protected surfaces that irrigation water rarely reaches. A trail of dried drops along the splash side suggests residue. Powdery islands growing across a leaf or onto a nearby petiole deserve a closer look, particularly if young leaves become distorted.

What to do now

Compare affected and healthy surfaces on the same plant. Photograph the coating before touching it, and leave the natural wax on shoots intact.

Inspect the edges with a magnifier for individual insects or cottony strands tucked into leaf joints. If the plant naturally has a blue-gray bloom, avoid rubbing it: fingerprints can remain visible long after the inspection. Where residue is plausible, observe leaves produced after switching to watering at soil level. Leave the original patch visible for comparison.

What to check next

If the coating reaches a swelling bud, photograph the bud before it opens and the leaves that emerge from it. This provides a clearer history than a picture taken after the whole shoot is coated.

If fresh leaves develop enlarging patches despite avoiding splashes, document both sides of a leaf and nearby stems. Identify the plant before selecting a fungicide; similar white deposits can have entirely different causes, and a product suitable for one host can injure another.

Use a clean leaf to track the returning coating

Photograph a clean emerging leaf and an already coated node. Deposits following dried spray droplets may stay sharply bounded. If no more spray is applied and a patch nevertheless expands or appears on the new leaf, investigate a fungal coating. Whether the material wipes off once is insufficient to distinguish residue from a surface-growing fungus.
