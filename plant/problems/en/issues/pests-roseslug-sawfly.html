Rose leaves turn papery while their veins stay green

Roseslug sawfly larvae graze the soft parts of rose leaves. At first the tissue may look translucent; later it dries brown. An established shrub can carry conspicuous scars even after the short-lived feeding stage has finished.

Where to look

Examine a partly damaged leaflet while it is still attached. Hold it against the light to see the pattern of thin tissue between tougher veins, then turn it to examine both surfaces.

What confirms it

Look for a yellow-green, caterpillar-shaped larva with an orange-toned head. Its color can blend with a healthy patch of leaf directly beside the scraped area.

Similar damage

Leafcutter bees remove clean arcs at the edge, while Japanese beetles may gather openly on roses. A web of intact veins with a soft green sawfly larva on it points to a different culprit.

What to do

Handpick a few larvae or wash them from accessible leaves. Retain foliage with substantial green tissue; removing every damaged leaflet can cost the shrub more leaf area than the insect did.

Follow-up

Inspect newly expanded leaves for fresh scraping. If only old brown windows remain and no larvae are found, continued leaf browning may represent drying of earlier damage.
