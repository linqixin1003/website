Holes forming in brown spots

The hole forms when the center of a brown spot drops out, and a brown rim often remains. A trail still covered by the leaf skin belongs with the miner guide page.

What to look for

Compare newer spots with older holes and inspect the brown rim around each opening.

Look for a brown spot with its centre still attached beside an older hole. A brittle centre separating along a brown rim supports tissue loss after spotting. Fresh green bite marks point you toward chewing damage instead.

Possible causes and next steps

Dead tissue can drop out and leave holes. Photographs of the progression help distinguish this from chewing.

How to tell similar problems apart

Inspect the edges of the holes. A dead rim and nearby spots with intact centres suggest tissue fell out; fresh torn edges can indicate chewing.

Find a leaf bearing both intact spots and holes. A loose dry center still hanging from a brown ring shows how a spot could become a hole. If the newest openings have green torn edges and no preceding dead patch, inspect for chewing insects or physical damage.

What to do now

Photograph early spots alongside mature holes. Look on leaf undersides for insects before deciding on pest control.

Check the same intact brown spot after wind or rain. Documenting the center falling out is stronger evidence than the final shape of an empty hole. If you request identification, include the plant name and both views of an early lesion while there is still green tissue around it.

What to check next

If buds, twigs or fruit also develop marks, include those symptoms when seeking identification.

Find a spot before its center falls out

Look along the same shoot for a brown spot with an intact center, one whose center is cracked, and a finished hole. That sequence supports tissue dying before the hole forms. Fresh chewing often removes green tissue directly, without leaving matching intact spots nearby.
