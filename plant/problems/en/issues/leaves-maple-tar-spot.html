Raised tar spots on maple

Glossy black islands develop within pale patches on maple leaves.

What to check first

Hold one attached leaf against a pale background. Look for a yellow-green zone around a sharply defined black patch. Depending on the maple and fungus, a patch may look like one drop of tar or a cluster of small black dots. Inspect more than one leaf before judging the pattern.

How to tell similar problems apart

The black material is part of the affected leaf surface. A broad black film that wipes away from a sticky leaf points instead towards sooty mold associated with honeydew. Brown tissue running in from dry margins, without raised black centres, calls for a different check.

What to do now

On an established maple, conspicuous late-season spots often have little effect on the tree's overall condition. Keep sound foliage on the tree. When leaves fall, gather affected litter where practical. Treating leaves once mature black patches have formed will not erase those marks.

What to check next

Next season note when the first pale spots appear and whether the tree otherwise develops a full canopy. Repeated early loss of many leaves, dying shoots or loose bark deserves a broader assessment. Photograph the whole crown as well as an individual spot in that case.

Compare attached and fallen maple leaves

At season’s end, look for raised black islands on attached maple leaves, then compare with recently fallen leaves from the same branch. Mark the tree species and extent of premature leaf fall. A black patch on bark or a neighboring plant needs its own identification.
