White campion flowers have violet-dusted anthers

Dark powder in a white flower is especially informative when it comes from the anthers. Compare a fresh bloom and a fading one on the same patch of campion.

What to look for

Look at the tip of each stamen inside an open white campion flower. In anther smut, violet to brownish-purple powder takes the place of pollen and may dust the petals nearby.

Check the pattern

Locate where the material starts. In a fading or rain-damaged bloom, petals themselves may brown or stick together. Anther smut is most conspicuous at the pollen-bearing structures while petals may still appear fresh.

What to do

Check several newly opened flowers on the same plant. Photograph the anthers in place as well as the leaves and whole flower. The identity of the campion matters when naming the associated fungus.

When to check again

For a garden record, mark affected stems and note whether later flowers show the same pattern. If you are collecting seed, check healthy flowering plants separately and verify that capsules actually mature.

Compare flowers in place

Look at the anthers of a second campion some distance from the affected clump. A stain transferred from another flower may sit on one petal edge, whereas repeated powder-filled anthers in a freshly opened bloom show where the material originated.

Inspect a flower that has only just opened. Purple dust present inside several fresh anthers carries more weight than a dusted petal on an old bloom. Tag the stem so that later observations concern the same individual in a mixed stand.
