Hard shelf with a white underside on a trunk

A woody shelf can remain on a tree after the season in which it first appeared. Inspect its connection to the tree before using its appearance to decide whether the standing trunk needs evaluation.

Inspect the plant

Locate the point where the shelf joins the wood. Artist's conk has a dull upper surface and a light pore surface beneath that can show brown marks when bruised. Note whether the shelf emerges from an old pruning wound, the base or a higher limb. Document changes in the surrounding bark without scraping the white pore layer for a test.

Distinguish similar signs

A shelf on a nearby stump and one attached to the trunk of a standing tree have different implications. Photograph the attachment without prying it loose.

Choose a response

A conk can signal decay inside the wood, even when the canopy is green. If the tree overhangs an occupied area, have an arborist evaluate it.

Follow up

Record other shelves at different heights, old wounds and changes in lean. Removing the visible shelf does not tell you how much supporting wood remains.

A detail to record

For shelves on a forked trunk, record which side of the fork carries each one. A single front view can make a conk on one stem appear attached to the junction supporting both.

If the shelf breaks away

If the shelf falls off, record whether it was attached to the trunk, a branch union or an old pruning wound. From a distance, note what lies beneath that part of the crown. A new shelf in the same place or a crack alongside the old attachment is worth showing an arborist. The remaining wood strength cannot be inferred from the size of the fallen shelf.
