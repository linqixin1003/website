Two-lined chestnut borer in declining oaks

The photograph shows the adult beetle on a leaf. It does not show dead branches. Inspect the crown for those.

The two-lined chestnut borer often attacks stressed oaks. Feeding beneath the bark interferes with transport in a branch or trunk. A stressed tree can have several problems at once, so the history of the site matters as much as finding a beetle.

Where to look

Inspect the crown from the ground and record whether dead foliage begins in upper branches. Check for recent excavation, drought, compaction or repeated loss of leaves that may have weakened the tree.

What confirms it

On a fallen or already dead branch look under loose bark for winding galleries packed with frass. Small D-shaped exit holes and a slender dark beetle with two pale back stripes are further clues.

Similar damage

A D-shaped hole also occurs with related borers. Confirm the host tree first; oak wilt and other causes of oak decline need separate evaluation, particularly if nearby trees are dying quickly.

What to do

Avoid peeling bark on living trunks. Have declining mature trees assessed, especially where dead limbs overhang paths, and address documented root-zone stress where practical.

Follow-up

Photograph the canopy from the same location during the growing season. Compare the advancing boundary of dieback rather than counting old holes that can remain visible for years.

Ask when the upper branches first declined and when nearby soil was disturbed. On an already fallen branch, photograph galleries under loose bark as well as any exit hole. Recording both the borer evidence and the earlier stress helps an arborist assess the remaining live crown.
