Black leathery ends on young tomatoes

A dark patch at the flower scar often appears on the first fruit to set. Its position and the recent watering pattern help distinguish blossom-end rot from decay that starts elsewhere.

What to look for

Turn the fruit over while it is still attached. A flat tan patch directly opposite the stem may darken and sink as the fruit enlarges.

Check the pattern

Compare several fruits on the first truss. The leathery area of blossom-end rot starts at the flower scar. A white patch on a sun-facing shoulder suggests sun injury instead.

What to do

Check moisture below the surface before watering. Dry spells and injured roots interrupt calcium delivery to expanding fruit, even where soil calcium is adequate. Keep moisture steady and mulch after watering.

When to check again

Remove badly affected fruit and inspect the next truss. Existing lesions cannot heal. If the problem continues despite steady moisture, have soil tested before deciding whether any amendment is worthwhile.

Which fruit should improve?

Mark one small, symptom-free tomato today. If it and younger fruit continue developing normally while an older black patch merely becomes drier, judge the watering change by the new fruit. In containers, check the centre of the root ball as well as the exposed top layer.

Check drainage as well as watering frequency. If a container stands in a sealed outer pot, the compost can remain saturated even when its surface looks dry. Correct persistent standing water instead of watering more often merely because the fruit shows a black patch.
