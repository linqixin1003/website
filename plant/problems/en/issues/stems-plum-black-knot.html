Hard black knots on plum and cherry twigs

The black lump is easiest to recognize after leaves fall. Its position matters: a knot near the end of a cherry twig allows a different pruning decision from one enveloping a plum's main trunk.

Inspect the plant

Follow the branch through the swelling. Black knot wraps the wood in a long, rough mass. Younger knots can be olive green and much less conspicuous. Inspect the entire length of the branch rather than stopping at the first lump. A second, smaller swelling closer to the trunk changes where a clean cut can be made.

Distinguish similar signs

Compare several twigs in winter when leaves no longer hide them. A smooth pruning collar confined to an old cut has a different shape.

Choose a response

On reachable branches prune well below the knot during dormancy and dispose of the cuttings. A knot on the trunk needs an arborist's assessment.

Follow up

Inspect nearby wild cherries and plums as well as last year's cuts. Small swellings can become obvious the following season.

A detail to record

If the plum has several trunks, start at one black knot and trace its branch down to the trunk before marking it for pruning. Crossing branches can make a healthy shoot appear to carry the knot.

Look past the most obvious black knot

A mature black knot is easy to spot on bare wood. Look several forks beyond it for smaller, smoother olive-colored swellings. Count those separately from old black knots and mark their positions. If an olive swelling darkens and enlarges in the next growing season, the early photograph makes it much easier to identify the sequence.
