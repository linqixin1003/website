An apple tunnel leads to the seeds

Brown crumbs outside an apple can hide an entrance much smaller than the damage inside. Follow the passage through one fallen fruit to see whether the seeds have been reached.

What to look for

Check the blossom end and the places where two apples touch. Crumbly brown material around a hole can mark the entrance of a codling moth larva.

Check the pattern

Slice one damaged apple along the tunnel. A distinct passage leading into the core, with chewed seeds, is a stronger clue than a hole alone. Apple maggots usually leave narrower winding trails through the flesh.

What to do

Collect fallen and infested fruit regularly while larvae are still inside. Check picked apples before placing them in storage, where a larva can leave the fruit and shelter in the container.

When to check again

Record the first fresh entry holes and the proportion of damaged fruit. Where damage recurs, consult local orchard guidance on pheromone monitoring and the timing of protection for the following crop.

Keep a cut fruit as evidence

Photograph the entrance before cutting. Then cut from the side so the tunnel, core and seeds appear in the same picture. If the passage stops shallowly, inspect another affected fruit before treating the entire crop as a core-feeding infestation.

Check the blossom-end recess on apples that look sound from the side. A larva can enter there and leave little obvious damage on the cheek. Inspect early drops as well as hanging fruit when estimating how much of the crop is affected.
