Longhorn beetles and holes in woody stems

The photograph shows no hole. On a branch or trunk, look for a round hole and fresh sawdust, and photograph those separately from this long-horned, white-marked beetle.

Inspect the specimen

See whether the opening is fairly round and whether fresh dust lies below it. A dark old hole with no dust may be from a previous year.

Check similar symptoms

Several insects make round exit holes. A hole alone does not identify the insect; record its position and any visible debris.

Choose a response

If you suspect Asian longhorned beetle, leave branches, logs and firewood where they are. In the United States report the sighting to USDA APHIS or your state plant health office; elsewhere contact your local plant health authority.

Track the next change

Report the location promptly. A photo of a suspected adult Asian longhorned beetle or of possible tree damage is useful even when you have seen only one of these signs.

Host-specific check

For each observation, note what you saw directly and which tree, if any, bore the damage. An adult found on a stone does not tell you which tree it came from.

Connect an adult sighting with injury

Leave debris undisturbed and photograph its position beneath the opening from the ground. If a large limb above a walkway is cracked or dying back, keep clear and arrange professional assessment.

Which photographs help identify a borer?

For an identification request, photograph the beetle from above and the side with its antennae visible and some indication of scale. Separately photograph the tree’s leaves, bark and any opening; record both dimensions of the opening. A confirmed adult sighting and a suspected exit hole are two observations. Neither alone justifies removing a tree with old holes.
