Winding pale trails inside leaves

The pale trail runs between the leaf surfaces and wanders. A hole that opens through to the light belongs with the shot-hole guide page.

What to look for

Hold a leaf to light. Look for a winding track between the surfaces, often broader toward one end.

Inspect the plant before washing or moving it. Start where the damage looks freshest and turn the leaf over without shaking the shoot. Include the attachment to the stem in your inspection. A hand lens or a close phone photo can reveal detail that a picture of the whole plant misses.

Possible causes and next steps

Leaf-mining insects feed inside leaves. Watch for fresh mines; lightly marked leaves can usually remain.

How to tell similar problems apart

A mine runs inside the leaf and often widens as it winds. A scratch on the surface lacks the enclosed tunnel.

Hold the blade to a light and follow one trail from the narrow beginning to its wider end. An occupant may be visible inside the wide portion. Empty tracks persist after emergence. Check younger leaves for occupied mines before removing numerous leaves with old tracks.

What to do now

Hold the leaf up to light and inspect the wider end for a larva or a dark trail. Leave enough healthy foliage if removing infested leaves.

Trace individual trails rather than counting every crossing as a new insect. A single winding mine can loop back over the same part of the blade. Save a close-up of any occupant alongside a picture showing the injury on the plant.

What to check next

Old empty mines do not disappear. Count new, narrow tunnels on recently expanded leaves to judge current activity.

Find the newest end of the mine

Follow a winding mine from its narrow beginning toward its wider end. Backlighting can reveal a dark frass line or a larva still inside the blade. If the wide end is empty and has an exit slit, inspect younger neighboring leaves for active mines before choosing any intervention.
