Pine branch junctions ooze coarse lumps of pitch

Zimmerman pine moth damage is easiest to recognize by its position on the tree. Inspect branch whorls on the main trunk as well as any broken top. A single lump of hardened resin on a random piece of bark has less diagnostic value than a pitch mass at a weakened junction.

Where to look

Walk around the trunk and inspect the whorls where branches join it. Pale masses of resin mixed with granular material are important clues on susceptible pines. View the same junction from two directions. A branch may hide the point where resin emerges, and material that has run down the bark can collect at a different spot from the original wound.

What confirms it

Look above each resin mass for a weak or broken leader. The caterpillar feeds under bark near a branch junction and can weaken this attachment.

Similar damage

Resin can also flow from a mechanical wound. Record the location and whether fresh granules are caught in it before identifying a moth.

What to do

Have a tree professional inspect a weakened leader over a walkway. Avoid cutting into the trunk to chase a hidden caterpillar.

Follow-up

Photograph individual whorls and revisit them for soft new pitch or fresh breakage. Old hard pitch can persist after feeding ends.

Photograph a branch whorl from the side so the pitch mass and its attachment to the trunk are both visible. Revisit that exact junction for new sticky pitch or a widening crack. A sound neighboring whorl with old hardened resin should be assessed separately from a failing leader.
