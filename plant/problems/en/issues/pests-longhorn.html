Longhorn beetles

Longhorn beetles and holes in wood

This is one possible explanation. Compare the signs below with those on your plant.

What to look for

Inspect wood for holes, debris and declining branches. A visiting adult alone does not confirm larvae in the tree.

Possible causes and next steps

Have a local arborist assess the host and holes. Avoid moving suspect wood.

How to tell similar problems apart

An adult longhorn beetle can land on healthy wood. Fresh boring dust and repeated holes on a declining branch give stronger evidence of a borer.

Observe the trunk and branches from the ground. An exit hole is a record of past emergence; by itself it tells you little about activity today. Photograph its outline with a ruler alongside, the material beneath it, and the surrounding canopy. Check whether loose material has simply fallen from a hole higher up. Bark damage from tools can also expose wood without an insect being involved.

What to do now

Photograph the hole, dust and surrounding bark. Avoid probing deep into the trunk or moving suspect firewood elsewhere.

Record the tree species if known and where in the canopy foliage has thinned. Old holes on a cut log and new dust on a living branch have different implications. Leave the bark intact and keep suspect wood on site while it is identified. If a large weakened branch hangs over a path, keep people clear and ask an arborist to assess its stability before attempting any pruning.

What to check next

Seek an arborist if a large limb weakens or fresh dust keeps appearing. Retain a photo of any adult for identification.
