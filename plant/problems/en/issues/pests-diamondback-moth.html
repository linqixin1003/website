Small windowpanes on cabbage leaves conceal wriggling larvae

Young diamondback moth larvae and other cabbage caterpillars can occur together. On a leafy crop, combining the shape of the earliest feeding marks with the movement of a live larva is particularly helpful. A large ragged hole on an old outer leaf seldom identifies the species on its own.

Where to look

Hold a young cabbage leaf against the light. Small pale windows appear where a larva has scraped the lower surface while leaving the upper skin intact. Inspect the small leaves forming around a broccoli head as well as the exposed leaves at the outside of the plant. Larvae concealed nearer the harvestable portion can be missed when a gardener examines only conspicuous holes at the edge.

What confirms it

Disturb a small tapered green larva gently. A diamondback moth larva often wriggles vigorously and may descend on a silk thread.

Similar damage

Check several caterpillars before deciding. Imported cabbageworms look velvety, and cabbage loopers arch the middle of the body as they walk.

What to do

On a small crop handpick visible larvae and examine the folded center leaves. Place insect netting over clean young plants and seal the edges.

Follow-up

Look for fresh windowpanes between the expanding leaves. Older windows tear into holes, which can exaggerate the apparent amount of recent feeding.

Examine the heart of each plant before deciding whether the outer holes matter. Live wriggling larvae on the leaves folding around a forming head are still within the harvest area. When these folds are clean and only old outer leaves are torn, inspect again before intervening.
