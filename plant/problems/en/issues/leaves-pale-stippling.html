Dense pale stippling

The flecks are dense and fine, mostly on the upper surface. Fine webbing and tiny animals on the underside belong with the mite guide page. Wider scattered spots belong with the pale-spot guide page.

What to look for

Inspect veins and leaf undersides for moving mites, fine webbing or cast skins.

Inspect the plant before washing or moving it. Start where the damage looks freshest and turn the leaf over without shaking the shoot. Include the attachment to the stem in your inspection. A hand lens or a close phone photo can reveal detail that a picture of the whole plant misses.

Possible causes and next steps

Several sap-feeding pests cause stippling. Confirm with a lens and separate infested pots.

How to tell similar problems apart

Compare exposed and sheltered leaves. Bleaching on the sunward face suggests exposure, while fine dots with insects underneath suggest feeding.

Hold white paper beneath a speckled leaf and tap gently. Examine anything moving on the paper with magnification, then inspect leaf undersides for webbing or shed skins. If nothing moves, repeat on a newly affected leaf before assuming a particular pest caused the stippling.

What to do now

Examine the underside and photograph the distribution of marks. Avoid immediately moving a shade-grown plant into full sun.

Keep the paper still while examining it. Movement caused by a breeze can make grains of dust look alive, particularly at high magnification. Save a close-up of any occupant alongside a picture showing the injury on the plant.

What to check next

Check whether new pale marks develop on sheltered foliage as well. If so, document any insects or residue you find.

Check whether speckles align above feeding sites

Choose a leaf with a clear patch of stippling and examine the reverse directly beneath it. Dark varnish-like deposits, shed skins or live lace bugs favor one explanation; fine webbing and crawling mites favor another. Stippling on the upper surface alone cannot separate them.
