Powdery mildew

White powder on plant surfaces

This is one possible explanation. Compare the signs below with those on your plant.

What to look for

Look for irregular powdery patches and check leaves, young stems and fruit.

Possible causes and next steps

Powdery mildew and surface residues can look similar. Note recurrence, allow airflow and identify the host.

How to tell similar problems apart

Natural wax often coats similar surfaces evenly. Powdery growth more often forms irregular patches that enlarge or appear on fresh tissue.

Angle the shoot toward the light and compare the coating on old and newly opened leaves. Look at protected surfaces that irrigation water rarely reaches. A trail of dried drops along the splash side suggests residue. Powdery islands growing across a leaf or onto a nearby petiole deserve a closer look, particularly if young leaves become distorted.

What to do now

Compare affected and healthy surfaces on the same plant. Photograph the coating before touching it, and leave the natural wax on shoots intact.

Inspect the edges with a magnifier for individual insects or cottony strands tucked into leaf joints. If the plant naturally has a blue-gray bloom, avoid rubbing it: fingerprints can remain visible long after the inspection. Where residue is plausible, observe leaves produced after switching to watering at soil level. Leave the original patch visible for comparison.

What to check next

If new patches spread to shoots or leaves, identify the host and seek advice on whether treatment is needed.

If fresh leaves develop enlarging patches despite avoiding splashes, document both sides of a leaf and nearby stems. Identify the plant before selecting a fungicide; similar white deposits can have entirely different causes, and a product suitable for one host can injure another.
