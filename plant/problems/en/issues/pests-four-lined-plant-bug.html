Mint leaves develop clusters of small sunken dots

Four-lined plant bugs puncture tender foliage, leaving small marks that may look surprisingly regular for insect injury. A patch of affected mint can retain these dots long after the bugs have gone. Base the diagnosis on the youngest affected leaves and on insects found among the nearby shoots.

Where to look

Inspect the freshest damaged foliage on mint or another herb. Small sunken spots can merge as the leaf expands; dried centers sometimes fall away. Inspect several neighboring stems before picking leaves for the kitchen. These active insects may move away from the first shoot you touch, so examining only a plucked leaf later can leave you with spots but no insect to identify.

What confirms it

Approach slowly and turn a shoot over above white paper. Look for a quick red or orange nymph or a yellow-green adult with four black stripes.

Similar damage

The spots can look fungal. Nearby feeding bugs and a flush of similar fresh dots on tender growth support insect injury.

What to do

For modest damage on an established perennial, leave healthy foliage in place. Remove badly marked herb leaves as needed at harvest.

Follow-up

Check the next flush of leaves. Earlier holes may enlarge with the leaf even when the short feeding period has ended.

Separate the oldest spotted leaves from the newest usable herb tips. If new leaves harden without dots while the original spots enlarge or crack, retain the new shoots. A fresh cluster of pinpricks on an expanding tip is the place to look for a bug before picking leaves.
