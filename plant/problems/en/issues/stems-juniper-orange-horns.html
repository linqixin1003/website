Orange jelly horns on juniper

An orange, jelly-like star appears on a woody swelling after rain.

What to check first

Follow one orange projection back to the twig without pulling on it. On susceptible junipers, cedar-apple rust can form a rounded woody gall bearing several soft horns. Include a healthy sprig in the photograph so the scale-like or needle-like foliage and the attachment of the gall are visible.

How to tell similar problems apart

Check again when the branch has dried. The projections can shrink dramatically while the woody gall stays in place. Sap usually runs down from a break; it does not repeatedly emerge as an orderly group of horns from pits in a round gall.

What to do now

A few galls on an otherwise vigorous juniper seldom justify cutting into a main branch. Photograph their positions. If a low, small twig carrying a gall is expendable, remove that twig with normal pruning cuts during dry weather. Leave larger structural pruning to someone experienced.

What to check next

A nearby apple or crabapple may later show orange leaf spots, but several related rusts involve different hosts. Record both host names separately and photograph each set of symptoms. Removing one garden juniper cannot guarantee that an apple will stay free of rust.

Finding the same gall in dry weather

Photograph the fork as well as the orange horns while they are conspicuous. In dry weather the horns shrink and may seem to vanish. Use the fork to relocate the woody swelling and compare its outline with the earlier photograph. When counting new galls, exclude flat patches of orange lichen that follow the bark surface.
