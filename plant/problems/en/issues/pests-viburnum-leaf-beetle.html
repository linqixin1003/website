Viburnum foliage is skeletonized beneath rows of twig scars

Viburnum leaf beetles leave evidence on two parts of their host. The foliage records the feeding of larvae and adults, while the youngest woody twigs can carry the next generation of eggs. A dormant shrub can therefore still provide evidence after the damaged leaves have fallen.

Where to look

Confirm that the shrub is a viburnum. In spring inspect the underside of skeletonized leaves for small elongated larvae with contrasting dark markings. Compare two twigs of similar thickness, one with a row of pits and one with smooth bark. Keeping a photograph of the intact row will help you recognize other infested shoot tips during winter pruning.

What confirms it

Examine the undersides of recent twigs near their tips. Small capped pits lined up along a twig are places where females have inserted eggs.

Similar damage

Adults later chew holes through leaves. Twig egg scars and the spring larvae distinguish the problem from assorted beetles visiting a flowering shrub.

What to do

During dormancy, prune out egg-infested twig tips where practical. On a small shrub check beneath leaves and remove accessible larvae.

Follow-up

Keep the same shrub under observation through summer and the following spring. Repeated heavy loss of foliage is more consequential than a few isolated holes.

Mark a capped row on a viburnum twig during dormancy. Check after bud break for young larvae feeding below newly expanded leaves. Once hatching has occurred, removing a twig with empty egg pits no longer addresses the larvae now feeding elsewhere on the shrub.
