Uneven lobes and scars on tomato fruit

A tomato can develop several unequal lobes around its flower scar. Notice when the shape first appears and whether the rest of the truss develops normally.

What to look for

The pictured tomato has pronounced horn-like lobes. Turn a similar fruit over and look for folds around the blossom end. In catfacing, lobes of otherwise firm fruit grow around deep creases. A healed brown seam may run down into a hollow.

Check the pattern

A growth crack usually starts near the stalk and splits the skin of an already expanding tomato. Catfacing changes the shape of the entire blossom end early in development.

What to do

Look back to conditions when that truss flowered. Large-fruited varieties are especially susceptible. A cold spell around bloom can explain a single affected truss, although appearance alone cannot pinpoint the trigger.

When to check again

Check the next flowers and the newest leaves. Widespread twisting on unrelated nearby plants warrants investigating spray drift. For repeated damage limited to one variety, compare a smaller-fruited cultivar next season.

Read the folds

Inspect the base of a second green fruit that has only just started expanding. A misplaced lobe already visible at that stage points to development in the flower. A new tear with moist edges across previously smooth skin points to a later injury.

Look inside the deepest crease when harvesting a catfaced tomato. A dry, closed seam and a damp cavity with collapsing flesh are different conditions. Keep fruit showing secondary decay aside when handling the rest of the harvest.
