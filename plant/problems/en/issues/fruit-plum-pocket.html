Young plums stretch into hollow pockets

Unripe plums that seem to be racing ahead of the rest can turn out to be nearly empty. The missing stone is easier to recognise before the pocket shrivels.

What to look for

Look at young fruit beside normally shaped plums on the same twig. An unusually long, soft, pale fruit that feels light for its size may be a plum pocket.

Check the pattern

Open one lengthwise. The centre may be empty where the stone should be forming. An insect-damaged plum generally still has recognisable fruit tissue around a tunnel or feeding site.

What to do

Remove affected fruit while the surface is still smooth. A pale coating can appear later as the fungus produces spores. Inspect adjacent fruit-bearing twigs for persistent swelling or unusually crowded shoots.

When to check again

Watch the tree from fruit set next spring. Marking the affected branch lets you see whether new pockets recur there or appear elsewhere. Isolated pockets seldom justify extensive pruning of healthy wood.

Compare like-aged plums

Choose two fruits of similar age and cut them in the same direction. Even before a healthy stone hardens, a normal plum has flesh around a developing centre. The elongated pocket may instead fold or collapse when you press its sides.
