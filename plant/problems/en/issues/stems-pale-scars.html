Pale marks that stay the same size

The pale marks are no longer enlarging. A brown spot that is still growing belongs with the stem-spot guide page. The same stable pale mark on a leaf belongs with the leaf guide page.

What to look for

Photograph the mark next to a ruler. Check whether the bark has closed around it and whether its edges change over time.

Possible causes and next steps

A rubbing branch, a tight tie or an old pruning cut can leave a pale scar. Remove the source of rubbing. If the area expands or the shoot wilts, check for active damage.

How to tell similar problems apart

Check whether the pale strip follows the edge of a support wire or sits opposite a crossing branch. A matching contact point explains its position better than the pale colour alone.

View the pale strip from the side as well as head on. Turn the pot gently to see whether the mark lines up with a support, pot edge or overlapping shoot. Look for a matching worn spot on the surface it touches. A stake may only make contact when air from a window moves the shoot.

What to do now

Loosen a tie that is pressing into the stem. Keep enough support for the shoot to stand without moving the tight loop onto a fresh section of bark.

If you find a contact point, create a small gap or reposition the support with a loose soft tie. Leave the scar exposed for observation. Cutting away the pale surface creates a new wound and makes comparison harder. Where insects are suspected, inspect nearby young leaves and sheltered folds with a lens before choosing any treatment.

What to check next

Compare the bark immediately beyond the old scrape as the shoot lengthens. A fresh line of abrasion in the same direction is a reason to check how the support moves in wind.

Use a leaf joint as a landmark in your photo. If the stem thickens, an old mark may stretch with it. Fresh streaks beyond the contact area, black specks on unfolding leaves or an edge turning soft call for a closer examination of those new changes.

Match the scar to the support

Watch the branch from the side when it moves and see whether it touches a stake, pot rim or neighboring stem at the scar height. Relieve the rubbing and inspect the next section of growth. Existing scars may widen as a stem thickens; a fresh mark on previously undamaged tissue is more informative than widening of the original scar.
