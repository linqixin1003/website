Misshapen fruit

The fruit is misshapen, and the skin may still be intact. Spots, mold, and soft rot are separate notes.

What to look for

Check for one-sided development and note flowering-time weather and any leaf symptoms.

Possible causes and next steps

Incomplete pollination, temperature changes and insect injury can alter shape. Compare later fruit of the same cultivar.

How to tell similar problems apart

Uneven seed development can leave one side small. Scars or punctures concentrated on the misshapen side point toward an injury while the fruit was young. Check more than one flower cluster, since a single distorted berry gives little evidence about the whole planting.

Look at the youngest fruit first, before ripening hides the original injury. On a strawberry, compare the spacing of the seeds on the swollen and undersized sides. Tissue develops around successfully pollinated seeds, so parts of a single berry can grow unevenly after a spell of cold, rainy weather at flowering.

What to do now

Compare several fruits from the same flowering period. Note cold or wet weather at bloom and inspect nearby young fruits for fresh feeding marks.

A hard tip crowded with seeds on several berries is a reason to inspect open strawberry flowers for plant bugs. Tap a flower cluster over a pale tray and photograph any small, fast-moving insects before they leave. A dark flower center after a frosty night provides another lead, even if neighboring flowers still look healthy.

What to check next

Established fruit will keep its shape. If the next batch is also distorted, photograph an open flower, a young fruit and a cut mature fruit for comparison.

Keep track of flowers opening in milder weather. If fruit from these flowers fills out evenly and scouting finds no active feeders, the timing helps narrow the cause of the earlier distortion.

For a misshapen strawberry, look at the tiny seed-like structures on its surface. A patch that failed to fill out can leave them crowded on one side. Compare that area with the well-expanded cheek before deciding whether the flesh was actually eaten away.
