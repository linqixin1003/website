Dimpled apples show brown winding tracks in the flesh

An apple may keep much of its normal outline while apple maggot larvae travel through the flesh. Internal browning can become easier to see as the fruit ripens. Compare the pattern inside several apples with the condition of their skins before deciding which fruit pest to manage.

Where to look

Examine several ripening apples for small dimples and soft patches. Cut across the flesh at more than one point to reveal fine brown trails. If a cut face looks clear, make another slice through a dimple on the same fruit. A single section can miss the winding track; examining the entire region helps distinguish a small surface bruise from internal insect injury.

What confirms it

Look in the damaged flesh for a pale, legless maggot. The winding route through the flesh is often easier to see than the insect itself.

Similar damage

A deep gallery packed with coarse frass at the core suggests codling moth. Apple maggot injury spreads through the flesh and can become conspicuous as fruit softens.

What to do

Pick up fallen fruit regularly so larvae have fewer opportunities to leave apples and enter the soil. Bag healthy fruit early on a small home tree.

Follow-up

Examine more than the visibly worst fruit at harvest. Record which trees show internal trails so monitoring can start before next season’s apples ripen.

Write down when each damaged apple was picked up. Larvae may have left a fallen apple by the time it is cut open. Slice a recently picked suspect apple from the same tree before deciding that empty trails in old drops mean the season's injury has ended.
