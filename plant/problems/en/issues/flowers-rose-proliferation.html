A new rosebud grows out of an open bloom

An apparently finished flower produces another bud through its centre. Following the base of the new bud helps tell true proliferation from overlapping flower stems.

What to look for

Look down into the flower cup before removing it. A new bud or short flowering shoot can emerge through the middle of an otherwise recognisable rose. Gardeners call this floral proliferation.

Check the pattern

Trace the new growth to its attachment. A second flower on a separate stem can simply lean over the first. Green leaf-shaped parts replacing several petals point to phyllody, which may accompany proliferation.

What to do

Photograph the open flower from the side and above. If only one bloom is affected and the plant is growing normally, deadhead as usual once the flower fades.

When to check again

Compare later flushes on the same cane and elsewhere on the bush. Repeated abnormal blooms along with distorted shoots deserve a closer assessment. Save photos showing the whole cane as well as the flower centre.

Follow the new bud

Look from below the bloom as well as from above. If two stems leave the cane separately, you are seeing neighbouring blooms. If the new bud emerges through the first flower's receptacle, mark the stem and see how far the second flower develops.

Wait until the first flower is fully open to count its centres. Tightly folded petals can hide the point where a new stalk emerges. A side photograph taken before the outer petals fall preserves evidence that deadheading would remove.
