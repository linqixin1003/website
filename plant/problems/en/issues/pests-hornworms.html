Tomato shoots stripped beside large dark droppings

A nearly bare tomato shoot can be the work of one well-camouflaged hornworm. The caterpillar often escapes an overhead inspection even when it is large enough to see clearly. Use its droppings to select a branch and examine the underside of that branch at eye level.

Where to look

Look below a stripped tomato shoot for chunky dark droppings. Follow them upward to the midrib, petiole or stem just behind the damaged tip. Hold the shoot still and scan it from the tip back toward the trunk. A caterpillar lying lengthwise against a petiole can mimic the outline of a leaf stalk until its pale side markings come into view.

What confirms it

A large green caterpillar can blend into a stem. Look sideways for pale diagonal or V-shaped markings and the horn at the rear.

Similar damage

Cutworms often sever young plants near soil level. Hornworms commonly leave an upright shoot with most of its leaf blades missing.

What to do

Handpick visible caterpillars on a small planting. Leave individuals bearing white, rice-shaped parasitoid cocoons so the beneficial wasps can emerge.

Follow-up

Reinspect nearby plants for fresh droppings and new bare petioles. One caterpillar can remain concealed even after another is removed.

Clear yesterday's droppings from directly beneath one stripped shoot and revisit the spot. Fresh pellets guide a second search even if the first caterpillar was removed. Trace them vertically toward the nearest remaining foliage; a larva can rest lengthwise on the shaded side of the stalk.
