Caterpillars chewing foliage

The photograph shows both the larvae and the holes. The hole edges are uneven, and the two larvae lie on leaf that has not yet been eaten through. Fine hairs cover them.

Inspect the specimen

Count the larvae and see whether the holes are fresh and whether green fragments lie beside them. Then check the underside and any rolled margin.

Check similar symptoms

A snail leaves a shining slime trail, and its holes are often cleaner-edged. Old holes with no larva are not evidence that a larva is still present.

Choose a response

Remove larvae when they are few, and leave leaf that can still catch light. Do not strip every leaf for a handful of holes.

Track the next change

The next day, see whether the holes have grown. If they have not, and the larvae are gone, stop.

Host-specific check

Use this page for these green larvae on a broad leaf. A silk nest in a branch fork is a separate note.

Look for the feeding stage on the plant

Open a folded leaf carefully over a tray and inspect bites, frass and any larva together. Count the fleshy abdominal prolegs if visible: sawfly larvae often have more than five pairs, while common caterpillars have at most five. Identification matters when selecting a control specific to caterpillars.

For a small vegetable planting, inspect the tender growing point as well as large damaged leaves. A few old holes in a mature outer leaf may matter less than one actively feeding larva hidden among the leaves that will form the head.
