Petals acquire ragged holes while daytime inspections find little

Earwigs shelter in tight spaces during the day and emerge to feed at night. They can chew tender ornamental growth and also prey on other garden insects. When a valued flower is being damaged, the timing of observation can decide whether a nearby earwig is responsible.

Where to look

Examine chewed petals and tender seedlings after dark with a flashlight. In daylight search folded petals, damp crevices and debris nearby for sheltering earwigs. Compare a newly opened flower with an older chewed bloom on the same plant. If the new petals remain intact over successive nights, finding earwigs in the older flower need not indicate ongoing damage to the crop.

What confirms it

Look for an elongated flattened insect with a conspicuous pair of forceps at its rear. Confirm feeding directly if possible because earwigs also eat other small insects.

Similar damage

Slugs can make similar holes and leave slime trails. Finding an earwig resting near a damaged flower by day is weaker evidence than seeing it feeding there at night.

What to do

If damage is continuing, place rolled corrugated cardboard near the affected plant as a daytime shelter. Check the roll in the morning and remove the occupants.

Follow-up

Inspect the next flowers as they open and compare the amount of new chewing. A small catch without fresh damage can simply reflect normal garden activity.
