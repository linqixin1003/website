A squash runner wilts above a stem hole with coarse frass

Squash vine borer larvae feed inside the runner, where they are sheltered from view even as the leaves above droop. Before adding more water to a wilted squash, check the lower stem for the material expelled from a tunnel. Compare the damaged runner with another from the same plant.

Where to look

Follow the wilted runner down to its base. Look along the stem for a hole with damp, sawdust-like frass pushed onto the stem or surrounding soil. If only one vine from a multi-stemmed plant is affected, follow that vine all the way back before inspecting the others. This keeps a symptomatic leaf connected to the exact length of stem that supplies it.

What confirms it

Examine a collapsed expendable stem lengthwise. A cream-colored caterpillar with a brown head may occupy the tunnel and interrupt water transport above it.

Similar damage

Heat can cause temporary midday wilt without a stem hole. Squash bugs often accompany egg clusters or nymphs beneath leaves; neither clue explains fresh stem frass.

What to do

Remove heavily damaged vines with larvae inside. For a healthy new planting, use covers before adult moths lay eggs and provide pollinator access at flowering.

Follow-up

Check other runners close to the ground. Watch for fresh frass before the whole vine collapses, since advanced wilt can come after substantial internal injury.

Brush loose frass from the outside of a suspect stem without enlarging the hole. Fresh material appearing at the same point is a useful reason to recheck that runner. If the stem has already collapsed, inspect the neighboring still-firm vines for earlier signs while they remain productive.
