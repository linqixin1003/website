Beetles on flowers

This page needs the beetle on the petal. Bite marks with no beetle stay off this page.

What to look for

Look for beetles actually chewing petals. Record their shape and when they visit.

Possible causes and next steps

Some flower visitors feed on pollen without harming petals. Confirm active chewing before managing the insect.

How to tell similar problems apart

Watch for a hard-bodied beetle actually chewing the plant. A beetle resting on a flower may be visiting for pollen. Include a side view of the head and legs if the beetle pauses on the rim.

Observe an undisturbed flower for a minute. Can you see a beetle taking bites along a petal edge, or is it moving between the pollen-bearing parts? On roses and peonies, rose chafers can leave broad, uneven gaps. Inspect leaves below the blossom for missing tissue between veins as a second clue.

What to do now

Look at fresh feeding edges and the undersides of adjacent leaves. Note whether damage is confined to petals or also affects new leaves.

If you find a cluster feeding on a small ornamental plant, hold a container beneath the bloom and gently dislodge the confirmed feeders. Leave other flower visitors alone. Record the beetle's color, leg shape and approximate length while it is still visible, since several species can cause nearly identical petal damage.

What to check next

Compare the next flush of leaves with a photo. If buds repeatedly fail to open, collect close-ups of both damage and beetle.

Inspect newly opened blossoms during the same part of the day. A few old ragged petals may remain conspicuous long after feeding stops. Count flowers with fresh bites to see whether the problem is still increasing.

Watch a beetle before removing it. One walking through pollen may leave the petals untouched; one chewing a fresh edge explains that particular notch. For a few damaged blooms, hand removal of a confirmed feeder avoids disturbing other flower visitors.
