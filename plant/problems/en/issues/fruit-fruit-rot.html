Soft, rotting fruit

The fruit is soft, wet, or collapsed. A fruit that is dry and still firm belongs with the shriveling guide page.

What to look for

Check whether softening begins near cracks, the stalk or the point touching the ground.

Possible causes and next steps

Wounds and prolonged dampness favor decay. Remove rotten fruit and allow air around healthy fruit.

How to tell similar problems apart

Press lightly beside the damaged area. Soft collapse can follow a bruise, chilling or decay; spreading softness is more concerning than a stable dent. Note whether the affected fruits were still attached when they softened or were already on the ground.

Find the point where the injury began. Look under the stalk, at the end opposite it and around cracks in the skin. Compare the same position on several fruits. For example, a dry, sunken patch consistently at the blossom end of tomatoes suggests blossom-end rot, a disorder linked to calcium supply in developing tissue.

What to do now

Separate a leaking fruit from sound fruit in the picking basket. Inspect the sides that touched it for broken skin before putting the rest away.

If that pattern fits, examine fluctuations in soil moisture during fruit enlargement before adding fertilizer. A different pattern, such as leaking flesh around a split after rain, calls for removing the affected fruit and checking nearby fruit for similar openings. An old dry injury can also become soft if decay enters later.

What to check next

On a fruit still attached, note whether the first soft patch surrounds the stalk, the blossom end or a split in the side. Compare the same location on the next fruit to show symptoms.

Keep a picture of the earliest stage alongside one showing later collapse. For an identification request, include the crop name and whether the patch was dry or wet when you first saw it.

On a cluster of soft fruit, trace each lesion back toward its earliest visible point. Repeated decay around stalk scars suggests inspecting harvest wounds, whereas matching patches on two touching fruits direct attention to the shared contact surface.

Several fruits softened at once. Did the damage start before picking?

Inspect fruit still on the plant and fruit from the top and bottom of the picking container. Soft areas restricted to flattened contact points in the bottom layer suggest handling damage played a part; use shallower layers and check the next picking. If attached fruit already shows expanding brown lesions, remove affected fruit from the plant and check fruit touching it. Keep decaying specimens apart from sound fruit during sorting.
